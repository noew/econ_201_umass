## Course Description (Catalog)

This course introduces methods for analyzing and drawing reliable conclusions from economic data. The goal is to develop an intuitive understanding of econometric techniques used in answering research questions in the social sciences, management, and public policy. The course emphasizes computation rather than formal derivations. Students will practice using statistical software to conduct data analysis, visualization, and report writing. 

**Prerequisites:** Students should have successfully completed one of the following courses (1) Math 127, Math 131, Econ 151 or Econ 152 AND one of the following (2) Res Econ 211, Res Econ 212, Stats 240, Stats 501 or Stats 515.

---

## Learning Goals

Upon completion of this course, you will be able to:

- Define the purpose and limitations of popular econometric techniques
- Fit, graph, and evaluate multivariate linear regression models
- Conduct a reproducible statistical analysis using statistical software

### Worksheets

Worksheets are designed to help you stay on track for the class, prepare you for the exams, and give you the tools to work on your own independent projects. You will encounter conceptual questions as well as applications using the computer. We encourage you to collaborate on solving the worksheets. However, your submission should be your own work. There will be approximately 6 graded worksheets over the course of the semester.

### Project

This project allows you to apply econometric methods to a research question of your choice, working collaboratively with peers. This assignment mirrors the type of empirical analysis conducted by professional economists and policy analysts. Groups of 3-5 students will be formed in Week 3. At the end of the semester, you will present your final project to the class in the form of a poster. Your group will also submit a brief write-up, approx. 4-6 pages on Canvas. A detailed description of expectations and a template document are available on Canvas.

### Quizzes

In-class quizzes are designed to check your understanding of key concepts and encourage active participation. These quizzes help you identify areas where you need additional study and provide immediate feedback on your learning.

- Quizzes will be administered during regular class sessions using the free Classquestion.com website
- Expect 1-2 multiple choice questions per week, typically administered at strategic points during the lecture.
- Quizzes cannot be made up, but your **lowest 3 quiz grades will be dropped** to account for absences and technical difficulties.

### Exams

Two in-person exams emphasize conceptual understanding as well as applications (without the need for a computer). For example, you may see the output from a statistical analysis and be asked to interpret the results. You will be given study guides to prepare.

- You may bring any physical notes to the exam, but you must refrain from communicating with others or using electronic devices.

### Free online textbooks

Completing the readings *ahead of time* for each class is a great strategy to maximize your learning. We draw on readings from the following two online textbooks (both are freely available):

- [Lessons in Statistical Thinking](https://dtkaplan.github.io/Lessons-in-statistical-thinking) by Daniel T. Kaplan (this text is referred to as *LST* in the course schedule)
- [The Effect: An Introduction to Research Design and Causality](https://theeffectbook.net/) by Nick Huntington-Klein (*Effect* in the course schedule)

### Free statistical software

In this class, we will learn how to use the computer for managing and analyzing data, and writing reports. You will need to have access to your own laptop computer with the following **free** software installed:

- **R**, a system for statistical analyses and graphics. This is like the *engine* of our car.
- **RStudio**, an integrated development environment (IDE), which provides an interface for working with *R*. This is like the *dashboard* of our car.

Instructions on downloading and installing these tools on your computer can be found on Canvas.

We will also use [DAGitty](https://dagitty.net/), a website for drawing and analyzing causal influence diagrams (DAGs). No installation is required.

### Acceptable use of Large Language models / Chatbots

We are at a very early stage of understanding how best to use Large Language models (LLMs) in teaching and learning. It seems clear (to me at least) that there are benefits, but also substantial downsides to using LLMs in your education (wrong answers, "hallucinations", undermining of learning). In our class, we will adhere to the following rules:

- If you choose to use an LLM for a *permitted* assignment, you need to include a prominent acknowledgment section at the end of your document. In this section, you need to specify *how* you have used the LLM in writing your assignment. For example, "I used the chatbot Claude to generate R code for this assignment, which I reviewed, edited, and take full responsibility for. All final wording reflects my own edits and judgment."
- LLMs may *not* be used during quizzes or exams.
